Engineering philosophy

I like hard problems that become simpler when the design is right.

These are the instincts I bring to software, architecture, AI systems, and engineering leadership.

Make the problem understandable before making the system powerful

I do my best work when I can turn an ambiguous problem into a clear model: what matters, what can change, what can fail, and what the system must make easy for everyone else.

  • Model the problem first
  • Separate signal from noise
  • Make tradeoffs explicit
  • Design for the next engineer

Fight complexity like it is the real production bug

Adding code is easy. Keeping a system understandable as it grows is the craft. I care about clean boundaries, deep abstractions, simple interfaces, and designs that future engineers can reason about.

  • Clean boundaries
  • Deep abstractions
  • Simple interfaces
  • Systems people can reason about

Architecture is leverage, not ceremony

Good architecture is not a diagram. It is the set of decisions that lets teams move faster, operate safer, and build on top of a system without rediscovering the same problems every month.

  • Decisions that compound
  • Faster team execution
  • Reusable foundations
  • Less repeated pain

Choose problems whose solutions compound

I am drawn to work where one strong system unlocks many future wins: automating weeks of work, operating large fleets, reducing cost for customers, improving engineering velocity, or turning a painful workflow into a repeatable path.

  • Automate weeks of work
  • Operate at fleet scale
  • Reduce customer cost
  • Create lasting leverage

How I build

A model response is not a system.

I am most interested in AI systems that survive contact with real engineering environments: unclear requirements, partial context, risky tools, long-running tasks, production constraints, and humans who need to trust the outcome.

State, validation, ownership, and recovery make AI useful in production.

Agents need contracts: inputs, outputs, handoffs, retry behavior, audit trails, and stop conditions.

Human approval should be designed into the workflow, not bolted on after risk appears.

The best automation reduces risk and cognitive load at the same time.
